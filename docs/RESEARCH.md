# When Does a Heuristic Help? Blind and Heuristic Search on Large Grid Mazes

**Anush Goel** · Research notes for Maze Search Lab · Version 1.0, October 2026

---

## Abstract

A* search is usually expected to explore far less of a search space than blind methods because a
heuristic steers it toward the goal. I tested that expectation on a 1,001 × 1,001 perfect maze
(499,999 open cells) and on 42 further generated mazes of three different textures, comparing
breadth-first search (BFS), depth-first search (DFS), A* and five variants. On the winding study
maze, A* with the Manhattan heuristic expanded only 1.3% fewer cells than BFS and took 1.8 times
as long. A simple counting argument explains the result: with a consistent heuristic, A* must
expand every cell whose true distance from the start lies more than the heuristic's maximum value
below the optimal cost, and here that leaves just 5,032 skippable cells (1.0% of the maze). How
winding a maze is predicts A*'s relative gain across all generated mazes (Spearman ρ = 0.94), and
adding loops reverses the ranking: A* fell to 31% of the cells while BFS still expanded them all.
Replacing the Manhattan distance with landmark lower bounds (ALT) cut A*'s work by a median factor
of 3.7 per query, kept every route optimal, and repaid its preparation cost after about 11 queries.
The general lesson is that a heuristic's value depends on how well it tracks the true remaining
cost, not on admissibility alone.

## 1. Motivation and research questions

I started this project after a simple surprise: on a large, winding maze, A* was the *slowest* of
the three classic algorithms I had implemented. Rather than treat that as an implementation
accident, I set out to explain it, test the explanation and find the conditions under which the
textbook ranking does hold. The work is organized around six questions:

- **RQ1. Correctness.** Do all implementations return valid routes, and the optimal ones shortest
  routes, on mazes with and without loops?
- **RQ2. Cost.** How do time, work (cells expanded) and memory compare on one large perfect maze?
- **RQ3. Mechanism.** Why does the Manhattan heuristic help so little there, and can the size of its
  possible saving be predicted before running A*?
- **RQ4. Structure.** How do loops, the position of the exit, DFS's move order, open space and the
  maze generator change the ranking?
- **RQ5. Bidirectional search.** Does searching from both ends at once help on such mazes?
- **RQ6. Better information.** Can a better admissible heuristic make A* pay off, and at what cost?

## 2. Background

A search problem consists of states, actions, a transition model, a goal test and step costs
(Russell & Norvig, 2021). In a grid maze the states are open cells, the actions are moves to the
four orthogonal neighbors, and every move costs 1. BFS expands cells in order of depth with a
first-in, first-out queue; the idea goes back to Moore's (1959) shortest path through a maze and
Lee's (1961) wave-propagation router. DFS follows the most recent branch with a last-in, first-out
stack (Tarjan, 1972). Both are *blind*: they use only the problem definition.

A* expands the frontier node with the lowest *f(n) = g(n) + h(n)*, where *g(n)* is the cost paid
so far and *h(n)* estimates the remaining cost (Hart et al., 1968). If *h* never overestimates
(admissibility), A* returns an optimal path; if, in addition, *h(n) ≤ c(n, n′) + h(n′)* for every
edge (consistency), A* never needs to reopen a node, and every node with *f(n) < C\** is expanded,
where *C\** is the optimal cost (Dechter & Pearl, 1985; Pearl, 1984). With *h = 0*, A* reduces to
uniform-cost search, which is Dijkstra's (1959) algorithm stopped at the goal. Multiplying *h* by a
weight *w > 1* gives weighted A*, which trades the optimality guarantee for greedier search
(Pohl, 1970).

The Manhattan distance |Δrow| + |Δcol| is the standard admissible and consistent heuristic for
4-connected grids. Landmark heuristics (ALT) instead precompute exact distances *d(L, ·)* from a
few landmark cells *L*. For any target *t*, the triangle inequality on an undirected graph gives
*d(n, t) ≥ |d(L, n) − d(L, t)|*, so the maximum over landmarks is admissible and consistent
(Goldberg & Harrelson, 2005). The bound follows the graph rather than the plane, which is why it
is the usual remedy when geometric bounds are weak, as in road networks with travel-time costs
(Bast et al., 2016).

## 3. Experimental setup

### 3.1 Mazes

All mazes are generated by the project itself, with fixed seeds, so the study needs no external
data. Three generators with very different textures are used (Buck, 2015):

| Generator | Character | Tortuosity at 1,001 × 1,001 |
|---|---|---|
| Recursive backtracker | long, winding corridors, few dead ends | 36.2–38.3 |
| Prim's algorithm | short corridors, many dead ends | 1.1–1.4 |
| Binary tree | every cell opens north or east; near-straight routes | 1.0 |

*Tortuosity* is the shortest route length divided by the Manhattan distance between S and E.

The **study maze** is a 1,001 × 1,001 recursive-backtracker maze (seed 2026) with S in the
top-left corner and E in the bottom-right corner. It has 499,999 open cells, 24,881 dead ends,
24,433 junctions and no loops: it is a *perfect* maze. Its only route is 76,360 moves long, 38.3
times the Manhattan distance of 1,996. It is stored as
`data/mazes/study_1001x1001_backtracker_seed2026.txt.gz` (91 kB).

### 3.2 Algorithms

Eight algorithms share one interface (`mazesearch.ALGORITHMS`): BFS, DFS, A* with the Manhattan
distance, bidirectional BFS, uniform-cost search (UCS), weighted A* (*w* = 2), greedy best-first
search, and A* with ALT bounds from 8 landmarks chosen by farthest-point selection. Every algorithm
is a graph search, so no cell is expanded twice.

### 3.3 Measurement protocol

- **Work** (cells expanded, frontier insertions, peak frontier size, path length) is exact and
  machine-independent. I treat it as the primary evidence.
- **Time** is measured around the search call only, with `time.perf_counter`. Each algorithm gets
  one untimed warm-up run; the seven timed runs are then interleaved round-robin across
  algorithms, so changes in background load affect all of them alike, and garbage is collected
  before each run. I report medians and interquartile ranges (IQR).
- **Memory** is the peak of Python allocations during one search, measured with `tracemalloc`
  (Python Software Foundation, n.d.) in separate runs, because tracing slows execution.
- **Rank correlations** use Spearman's (1904) coefficient.

All runs used CPython 3.12.3 on one core of a 2.10 GHz Intel Xeon processor under Linux. The
complete pipeline (`scripts/run_experiments.py`) took 353 s.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 RQ1: Correctness

On 24 seeded mazes of all three textures, with between 0% and 100% of removable walls opened and
random exits, every algorithm returned a valid route, and the five optimal algorithms (BFS,
bidirectional BFS, UCS, A* and ALT A*) always matched the BFS route length. A separate run over 60
such mazes found no mismatches either. The landmark heuristic showed no admissibility or
consistency violation at any cell of the test mazes. These checks are part of the test suite
(`tests/test_algorithms.py`, `tests/test_analysis.py`).

On the study maze, all eight algorithms returned the same 76,360-move route, including DFS, greedy
search and weighted A*, which carry no optimality guarantee. This is a property of the maze, not of
the algorithms:

> **Proposition 1.** In a perfect maze, every complete graph search that never revisits a cell
> returns the shortest route.
>
> *Proof.* The corridor graph of a perfect maze is a tree, so exactly one simple path joins S and E.
> A graph search that records reached cells extends each cell's path by one new cell at a time, so
> the route it returns is simple, and therefore it is that unique path. ∎

The practical consequence is that perfect mazes cannot reveal differences in optimality, only in
effort; Section 4.4 adds loops to expose them.

### 4.2 RQ2: Cost on the study maze

**Table 1.** *Eight algorithms on the 1,001 × 1,001 study maze* (`data/results/benchmark.csv`)

| Algorithm | Cells expanded (% of open) | Peak frontier | Peak memory (MiB) | Median time, s (IQR) | µs per expansion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BFS | 371,598 (74.3%) | 32 | 63.4 | 0.558 (0.018) | 1.50 |
| DFS | 360,416 (72.1%) | 1,622 | 64.1 | 0.543 (0.018) | 1.51 |
| A* (Manhattan) | 366,588 (73.3%) | 34 | 109.5 | 0.992 (0.051) | 2.71 |
| Bidirectional BFS | 400,248 (80.1%) | 58 | 77.0 | 0.767 (0.054) | 1.92 |
| UCS | 371,628 (74.3%) | 31 | 109.5 | 0.980 (0.005) | 2.64 |
| Weighted A* (*w* = 2) | 361,941 (72.4%) | 33 | 109.5 | 1.005 (0.019) | 2.78 |
| Greedy best-first | 150,952 (30.2%) | 215 | 20.6 | 0.331 (0.014) | 2.19 |
| A* + ALT (*k* = 8) | 145,898 (29.2%) | 1,131 | 33.4 | 0.565 (0.040) | 3.87 |

*Note.* All routes are 76,360 moves long (Proposition 1). ALT times exclude the 4.3 s needed to
prepare the landmark tables, which can be reused across queries (Section 4.6).

Time decomposes into work multiplied by the cost of one expansion. A* did 98.7% of BFS's work, but
each expansion cost 1.8 times as much, because every new cell needs a heap push and a heuristic
call, so A* finished 1.8 times slower. The run-to-run spread was small (IQR at most 7% of the
median), so the ranking is not noise.

Memory contradicts the usual intuition about the frontier. BFS's queue never held more than 32
cells and A*'s heap never more than 34, while DFS's stack peaked at 1,622 entries. The study maze
is deep and narrow: its 499,999 cells are spread over 99,783 distance levels, about five per
level, so each BFS ring is tiny, while DFS leaves unexplored siblings on its stack at every junction of its
dive. What dominates memory is the table of reached cells that graph search keeps; A* and UCS need
the most because they also store a cost and a closed flag for every reached cell.

### 4.3 RQ3: Why the heuristic barely helps

The size of A*'s possible saving can be bounded before running it.

> **Proposition 2 (pruning band).** Let *h* be consistent, let *g\*(n)* be the true distance from S
> to *n*, and let *h_max* be the largest value of *h*. Then A* expands every cell with
> *g\*(n) < C\* − h_max*, and the only cells it can avoid among those BFS must expand are the cells
> with *C\* − h(n) ≤ g\*(n) < C\**.
>
> *Proof.* With a consistent heuristic, A* expands every cell with *f(n) = g\*(n) + h(n) < C\**
> before it selects the goal (Hart et al., 1968; Dechter & Pearl, 1985). Since *h(n) ≤ h_max*,
> every cell with *g\*(n) < C\* − h_max* satisfies that condition. BFS, which tests the goal when it
> is generated, expands every cell with *g\*(n) ≤ C\* − 2* and part of the layer at *C\* − 1*, so the
> cells BFS expands but A* may skip are those closer to S than E is with *g\*(n) + h(n) ≥ C\**. ∎

On the study maze, *C\** = 76,360 while the Manhattan distance never exceeds 1,996. Computing the
exact distance of every cell (`mazesearch.analysis.heuristic_profile`) gives:

| Quantity | Cells |
|---|---|
| Closer to S than E is (what BFS must expand) | 371,610 |
| A* must expand (*f < C\**) | 366,578 |
| A* may expand (*f ≤ C\**) | 366,641 |
| Prunable band | 5,032 (1.0% of the maze) |
| **Measured:** BFS expanded / A* expanded | 371,598 / 366,588 |

A* expanded 366,588 cells, inside the window [366,578, 366,641] predicted by the theory. Its
measured saving over BFS (5,010 cells) is almost exactly the size of the band (5,032). The
heuristic is admissible and consistent, so it guarantees an optimal route, but at the start it
estimates 1,996 moves against a true 76,360 (2.6%), and along the route it captures on average
3.1% of the true remaining distance. In a maze this winding, the straight-line
distance says almost nothing about which branch leads to E.

A heuristic ablation on the same A* code confirms this (`heuristics.csv`):

| Heuristic | Cells expanded (% of BFS) | Must expand (*f < C\**) | Median time (s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *h* = 0 (uniform-cost) | 371,628 (100.0%) | 371,610 | 0.94 |
| Euclidean | 367,698 (99.0%) | 367,696 | 1.02 |
| Manhattan | 366,588 (98.7%) | 366,578 | 1.03 |
| Weighted, *w* = 2 | 361,941 (97.4%) | n/a | 1.00 |
| Weighted, *w* = 5 | 356,317 (95.9%) | n/a | 0.94 |
| Greedy-like, *w* = 10⁶ | 152,792 (41.1%) | n/a | 0.42 |
| ALT, *k* = 4 | 150,350 (40.5%) | 10,538 | 0.52 |
| ALT, *k* = 8 | 145,898 (39.3%) | 10,538 | 0.58 |
| ALT, *k* = 16 | 138,924 (37.4%) | 20 | 0.70 |

The ranking among the classical heuristics follows the dominance result (Manhattan ≥ Euclidean ≥ 0,
so fewer expansions), but the spread is about 1%. Only two things change the picture. Extreme
weighting approaches greedy search and gives up any guarantee; it happens to be safe here only
because Proposition 1 applies. The landmark bound reduces the forced work from 366,578 cells to
10,538 (*k* = 4) and to just 20 (*k* = 16) without giving up anything.

### 4.4 RQ4: How maze structure changes the ranking

**Loops.** Opening a random share of the 325,611 removable walls, those that separate two corridors
in a straight line, adds loops (`loops.csv`, means of three seeds):

| Walls opened | Shortest route | DFS route ÷ shortest | BFS expanded | A* expanded | A* time vs BFS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 76,360 | 1.00 | 74.3% | 73.3% | 1.9× slower |
| 0.1% | 22,645 | 2.13 | 91.8% | 89.9% | 1.9× slower |
| 1% | 6,576 | 3.57 | 99.5% | 97.7% | 2.1× slower |
| 10% | 2,367 | 11.17 | 100.0% | 79.3% | 2.0× slower |
| 20% | 2,042 | 10.98 | 100.0% | 31.1% | 1.3× **faster** |

Shortcuts make the route shorter, so it approaches the Manhattan bound, and the heuristic becomes
informative. DFS stays the fastest because it stops at the first route it finds, but that route
quickly becomes an order of magnitude too long. BFS remains optimal but expands nearly everything,
because the exit sits in the far corner. A*'s advantage appears only once the route is close to
the straight line.

**Exit position: a census of every exit.** Placing the exit somewhere else is the second most
consequential change, and for blind search it can be measured exhaustively:

> **Proposition 3 (goal independence of blind search).** With a fixed successor order, BFS and DFS
> expand cells in an order that does not depend on the goal; the goal only decides when they stop.
> Hence DFS reaches exit *g* after expanding exactly the cells it expands before *g*, and BFS
> (which tests the goal on generation) reaches *g* while expanding *g*'s earliest-expanded
> neighbor.
>
> *Proof.* The goal test is the only place the goal enters either algorithm, and it does not alter
> the frontier; the expansion sequence up to the stopping point is therefore identical to the
> sequence of a run with no reachable goal. ∎

Two complete traversals therefore give the cost of all 499,998 possible exits
(`mazesearch.analysis.exit_census`). I verified this against 31 real searches with zero
mismatches. BFS's cost is a deterministic function of the exit's distance from S (ρ = 1.00); DFS's
is far less predictable (ρ = 0.53). DFS beats BFS for 62.1% of exits, with a median cost ratio of
0.86. A*'s cost stayed within 6% of BFS's on the 31 sampled exits (median ratio 0.993). The app's
"DFS luck map" shows where in the maze DFS would be lucky.

**DFS move order.** The order in which DFS tries its moves is a hidden heuristic. Across all 24
orders, DFS expanded between 150,526 (S-E-W-N) and 425,830 (N-W-E-S) cells on the same maze.
Orders that try south before north, toward the exit, needed a median of 217,245 cells against
359,111 for the rest (`move_orders.csv`).

**Open space.** Removing every interior wall leaves a 999 × 999 room in which the Manhattan
distance is exact. A* then expanded only the 1,996 cells of its route in 0.09 s, while BFS expanded
997,999 of the 998,001 open cells. DFS was either perfect or disastrous depending only on its move order: it
found the optimal route with north-east-south-west, and a 998,000-move route that visits every
cell (500 times the shortest) with north-west-south-east (`open_room.csv`).

**Texture and size.** Across 42 mazes (three generators × seven sizes from 51 to 1,001 × two
seeds), tortuosity ranks A*'s relative work almost perfectly (Spearman ρ = 0.94 between tortuosity
and A* work ÷ BFS work). The median ratio is 0.98 for backtracker mazes, 0.30 for Prim mazes and
0.015 for binary-tree mazes (`scaling.csv`). Log-log fits of work against maze size give slopes
between 0.98 and 1.02 for BFS on every generator, which is linear growth, as expected for graph
search. For A* and DFS on binary-tree mazes the slope is 0.51, because there both walk almost
straight to the exit, and the route length grows with the square root of the number of cells.

### 4.5 RQ5: Bidirectional search

Bidirectional BFS expanded 7.7% *more* cells than BFS on the study maze, although on road
networks it typically visits about half as many vertices (Bast et al., 2016). A growth argument
reconciles the two observations. If the number of cells within distance *r* of a point grows like
*r^k*, one search to radius *C\** costs about *C\*^k*, and two searches to radius *C\*/2* cost
about *2 (C\*/2)^k = 2^{1−k} C\*^k*. That is a saving only when *k > 1*. Road networks are roughly
planar (*k* ≈ 2), so the saving is about half; a perfect maze is a tree whose reachable region
grows roughly linearly with distance (*k* ≈ 1), so the saving disappears and the overhead remains.
With loops, the maze becomes more grid-like and bidirectional BFS did better: it expanded 66–80% of
the cells while BFS expanded 92–100%.

### 4.6 RQ6: Landmarks, a better heuristic

I prepared 16 landmarks by farthest-point selection (Goldberg & Harrelson, 2005), each costing one
complete BFS, and ran 30 random start and exit pairs on the study maze (`landmarks.csv`):

| Landmarks | Preparation (s) | Work saved per query (median, range) | Query speed-up (median) | Break-even (queries) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 2.2 | 3.2× (1.8–8.7) | 2.5× | 6 |
| 8 | 4.3 | 3.7× (2.0–8.7) | 2.5× | 11 |
| 16 | 8.5 | 3.9× (2.2–8.7) | 2.1× | 26 |

Every landmark query returned a route of exactly the same length as Manhattan A*, as admissibility
requires. More landmarks always reduce work, but beyond eight the extra table lookups in every
heuristic call cost more time than the saved expansions are worth. For a single query, plain A*
remains cheaper; from about a dozen queries on the same maze, the landmark tables pay for
themselves.

## 5. Design decisions and why

- **No recursion anywhere.** The study maze's route is 76,360 moves deep, far beyond Python's
  default recursion limit of 1,000. DFS uses an explicit stack and marks cells when they are popped,
  which reproduces the visiting order of recursive DFS; Proposition 3 relies on that order.
- **Early goal test in BFS.** BFS checks for the goal when a cell is generated, which is safe with
  unit costs and saves a whole ring of expansions (Russell & Norvig, 2021).
- **Tie-breaking and lazy deletion in A*.** Grids produce many ties on *f*. Breaking them toward
  the smaller *h* keeps the search moving toward the goal, and outdated heap entries are skipped
  when popped instead of being removed eagerly.
- **Graph search everywhere.** Every algorithm records reached cells, so none can loop, and the
  comparison measures search strategy rather than duplicate work. Iterative deepening (Korf, 1985)
  was ruled out because it would repeat a search more than 76,000 levels deep many times.
- **Flat integer arrays for landmark tables.** An `array('i')` uses 4 bytes per cell, about a tenth
  of a dictionary keyed by cells. Sixteen tables for a million-cell grid then fit in 64 MB.
- **Work counts as primary evidence.** Expansions are exact and machine-independent; timings
  depend on the interpreter and the processor and are reported with their spread.
- **Interleaved, warmed-up timing.** Rotating through the algorithms spreads background noise
  evenly; medians and IQRs resist the occasional slow run.
- **Resumable, seeded pipeline.** Every random choice is seeded and every stage can be rerun on its
  own (`--only`), which also made the study robust to interrupted runs.
- **Storage.** Results take 338 kB in total. The study maze is gzipped from about 1 MB to 91 kB with
  a fixed header, so the bytes never change. Maps for the app are downsampled by a factor of two
  and quantized to 8 bits, and every chart is drawn at runtime rather than stored as an image.

## 6. Threats to validity

- **Interpreter constants.** CPython makes heap operations and function calls relatively
  expensive, which inflates A*'s cost per expansion. In a compiled implementation the time gap
  would shrink, but the work counts, the main evidence, would not change.
- **One machine.** All timings come from one core of one processor; other hardware will give
  different seconds. The IQRs show low noise on this machine, and the counts reproduce exactly.
- **Generated mazes only.** Three generators, unit costs and 4-connected moves cover very different
  textures but not weighted terrain, diagonal moves or real maps; benchmark collections such as
  Sturtevant's (2012) would test generality further.
- **Corner placement.** S and E sit in opposite corners, which is the hardest case for BFS. The exit
  census covers every other exit position for the blind algorithms, but A* was sampled at 31 exits.
- **Landmark selection.** Only farthest-point selection was tested; other strategies, and symmetry
  pruning such as Jump Point Search on open grids (Harabor & Grastien, 2011), could do better.

## 7. Conclusions

| Claim | Evidence | Where to check |
|---|---|---|
| All implementations are correct; the optimal ones always return shortest routes. | 24 + 60 random mazes, zero mismatches; admissibility and consistency hold at every cell tested. | `tests/`, Section 4.1 |
| On a winding perfect maze, A* saves about 1% of BFS's work and is slower. | 366,588 vs 371,598 cells; 0.99 s vs 0.56 s. | `benchmark.csv`, app page 2 |
| That saving is predictable from the maze alone. | Prunable band of 5,032 cells vs 5,010 saved; A* inside the theoretical window. | Proposition 2, `heuristics.csv`, app page 3 |
| A heuristic's value depends on how closely it tracks the true cost. | Tortuosity ranks A*'s gain (ρ = 0.94); loops and open space make A* decisive. | `scaling.csv`, `loops.csv`, `open_room.csv`, app page 4 |
| DFS's speed is luck, and its routes degrade badly once loops exist. | 2.8× spread across move orders; routes 11× too long with loops; exit census ρ = 0.53. | `move_orders.csv`, `loops.csv`, census |
| Bidirectional BFS needs fast-growing neighborhoods. | 7.7% more work than BFS on the tree-like maze; less work once loops are added. | Section 4.5, `loops.csv` |
| Better information fixes A* without losing optimality. | ALT (*k* = 8): 3.7× less work and 2.5× faster per query; break-even after about 11 queries. | `landmarks.csv`, app page 5 |

Taken together, the results support one practical rule. Use BFS when an optimal route is needed and
no informative heuristic exists. Use DFS only when any route will do. Use A* when the heuristic
tracks the true cost, and when it does not, invest in a better heuristic, such as landmarks, rather
than in a cleverer search loop.

## 8. Reproducing this work

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
python scripts/run_experiments.py      # all eight stages, about 6 minutes
python -m pytest -q                    # correctness checks
streamlit run app/Home.py              # explore the results
```

Counts reproduce exactly; timings depend on the machine. `data/results/manifest.json` records the
environment, the seed (2026) and the run time of every stage.
